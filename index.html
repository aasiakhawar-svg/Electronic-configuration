Hotel Atomia

Where electrons check in. Follow one night at the hotel to learn how shells, orbitals, Hund’s rule and the Pauli principle decide who sleeps where.

✏️ Board designed by Teacher Aasia Khawar

1

The hotel has floors

Quantum shells are fixed energy levels. Ground floor is closest to the nucleus and cheapest in energy, so guests fill it first.
2

Each floor has room types

Floor 11s
Floor 22s2p
Floor 33s3p3d
Floor 44s4p4d4f
Sub-shells have slightly different energy: s < p < d < f on the same floor. Sub-shells are the room types, and orbitals are the individual rooms.
Orbital: a region of the atom that holds up to two electrons with opposite spins.
3

Room shapes

Rooms are drawn so there is a 90% chance of finding the electron inside. The three p rooms differ only in orientation.
4

How many guests fit?

Sub-shellRooms × 2
s1 × 2 = 2
p3 × 2 = 6
d5 × 2 = 10
f7 × 2 = 14
Add the sub-shells and you get the floor totals: 2, 8 (2+6), 18 (2+6+10), 32 (2+6+10+14).
5

Hund’s rule: bus-seat manners

“Nobody shares a room until every room on the floor has one guest.”
C: 1s22s22px12py1
Orbitals in a sub-shell each take a single electron before any pair up.
6

Pauli: sharing needs opposite spins

Two electrons in one room must spin opposite ways, shown as ↑ and ↓. Oxygen has 2 paired rooms plus 2 single guests in 2p, fluorine has one single, and neon is full.
7

Plot twist: the 4s room opens first

K: 1s22s22p63s23p64s1 (not 3d1)
Ca: …3p64s2
For K and Ca the 4s room has lower energy than 3d, so it fills first. From Sc onward 3d fills, and when these atoms ionise, the 4s electrons leave first.
Cr: …3d54s1 (not 3d44s2)
Cu: …3d104s1 (not 3d94s2)
Aufbau (fill lowest energy first) works for the first 36 elements except Cr and Cu. It describes the pattern but does not explain the exceptions.

Practice zone: lock in every concept

A. Quick-fire questions

Tap each question to reveal the answer.

1. How many electrons can one orbital hold?Two, with opposite spins.
2. How many orbitals are in an s, p and d sub-shell?1, 3 and 5.
3. How many electrons fit in the third quantum shell?18 (2 + 6 + 10).
4. Which has slightly higher energy, 2s or 2p?2p.
5. Why is helium 1s2 and not 1s12s1?The 1s orbital can take two electrons and has lower energy than 2s, so it fills first.
6. State Hund’s rule.Electrons occupy orbitals singly before pairing takes place.
7. State the Pauli exclusion principle.Two electrons cannot occupy the same orbital unless they have opposite spins.
8. Give the configuration of calcium.1s22s22p63s23p64s2. The 4s orbital is lower in energy than 3d here.
9. When scandium ionises, which electrons go first?The 4s electrons, which have the greater energy.
10. Which two elements among the first 36 break the usual filling pattern?Chromium (3d54s1) and copper (3d104s1).

C. Spot the mistake

Each “check-in” breaks a rule. Which one, and what is the fix?

  1. Carbon: 2p boxes drawn ↑↓, empty, empty
  2. Oxygen: one 2p box drawn ↑↑
  3. Potassium: 1s22s22p63s23p63d1
  4. Copper: …3d94s2
  5. Helium: 1s12s1
  6. Calcium: …3p63d2
Reveal the fixes
  1. Breaks Hund’s rule. Use 2px12py1.
  2. Breaks Pauli. Two ↑ cannot share a box, so one must be ↓.
  3. 4s fills before 3d. Correct: …3p64s1.
  4. Exception. Correct: …3d104s1.
  5. Lowest energy first. Correct: 1s2.
  6. Correct: …3p64s2.

D. Checkpoint 1 rehearsal

Based on the textbook checkpoint:

  1. Explain Hund’s rule and the Pauli exclusion principle in your own words.
  2. In a box diagram, what do the arrows mean, and what do x, y and z mean?
  3. Why is the 2py electron not in the 2px orbital?
  4. Describe the shapes of s and p orbitals.
  5. Draw the box configuration of sulfur, phosphorus and chromium.
Model answers
  1. Hund: orbitals in a sub-shell fill singly first. Pauli: two electrons share an orbital only with opposite spins.
  2. Arrows are electrons and their spin direction. x, y and z are the axes that show each p orbital’s orientation.
  3. 2px already holds one electron, and Hund’s rule says each orbital takes one before pairing.
  4. s is spherical. p is an elongated dumbbell, three at right angles.
  5. See panel 8 on the board above.

E. Hands-on classroom games

  1. Human hotel: chalk floors and rooms on the floor. Students are electrons holding ↑ or ↓ cards and “check in” element by element, from H to Kr. The class calls out any rule that is broken.
  2. Bus-seat challenge: set out pairs of chairs as p orbitals. Students board as N, then O, and see why singles come first, then pairs.
  3. Egg-carton orbitals: each cup is an orbital and two beads in red and blue are the spins. Build 2p, 3d and 4s for Fe, Cr and Cu.
  4. Balloon p orbitals: twist long balloons into dumbbells and tie three together at right angles to model px, py and pz.
  5. Clay shapes: mould a 1s sphere and a bigger 2s sphere, then three p dumbbells in different colours.
  6. Configuration bingo: call the element, and students mark its configuration on a card of notations.
  7. Mistake hunt relay: teams run to the board, find the wrong configuration and fix it. Score a point for naming the rule broken.
  8. Card sort: match element, atomic number, s,p,d notation and box diagram cards for the first 36 elements.
  9. Ionisation role-play: a student acting as Sc removes the 4s “guests” first, to show which electrons leave.
  10. Draw a comic: students create their own 4-panel story for one rule, like Hund’s rule on a bus, and swap with a partner.
  11. Periodic table colouring: colour s, p and d blocks and link each block to the sub-shell being filled.
  12. Exit ticket: on a sticky note, write the config of one element chosen at random from H to Kr and add the rule you used.
Differentiation: start with H to Ne (boxes only), move on to Na to Ar, then challenge with K to Kr including the exceptions Cr and Cu.
